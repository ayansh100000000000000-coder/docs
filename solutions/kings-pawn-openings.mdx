These are the solutions to [Chapter 2](/puzzles/kings-pawn-openings) of _The Permanent Advantage_. Three of these puzzles show a move that was not the best choice; for those, the solution also shows what you should have played instead. The ratings are teaching judgments from opening theory and game commentary, not engine evaluations.

## Puzzle 6: Anderssen vs Kieseritzky

London, June 21, 1851 (the "Immortal Game")<br /> King's Gambit Accepted, Bishop's Gambit (Bryan Countergambit). Result: 1-0 (23 moves)

<Warning>
  **Played in the game: 4...b5?!** Dubious (?!)
</Warning>

**Game line:** 4...b5 5.Bxb5 Nf6 6.Nf3 Qh6

Black gives a pawn to deflect the bishop and defend the f4 pawn, but the idea does not hold up well and development falls behind. Modern theory does not trust this countergambit. The game is immortal because of Anderssen's attack, not because of Black's opening. Standards in 1851 were different from today's.

## Puzzle 7: Morphy vs Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard

Paris Opera box, autumn 1858 (exact date uncertain)<br /> Philidor Defence. Result: 1-0 (17 moves)

<Warning>
  **Played in the game: 3...Bg4?!** Inaccuracy (?!)
</Warning>

**Game line:** 3...Bg4 4.dxe5 Bxf3 5.Qxf3 dxe5 6.Bc4 Nf6

Pinning the knight this early gives up the bishop for a knight and leaves Black behind in development after dxe5. Modern players prefer 3...exd4 or 3...Nf6. The move was accepted theory in 1858, so it is a historical inaccuracy, not a blunder.

## Puzzle 8: Capablanca vs Marshall

Manhattan Chess Club, New York, October 23, 1918<br /> Ruy Lopez, Marshall Attack. Result: 1-0 (36 moves)

<Check>
  **Played in the game: 9.exd5** Good move (main line)
</Check>

**Game line:** 9.exd5 Nxd5 10.Nxe5 Nxe5 11.Rxe5 Nf6

White accepts the pawn, which is the principled answer to Marshall's new idea. After 11.Rxe5 White is a pawn up and Black needs real compensation. Capablanca defended accurately and won. Today Black usually plays 11...c6 instead of Marshall's 11...Nf6. White can also avoid the whole gambit with an Anti-Marshall setup such as 8.h3 or 8.a4, which is a matter of style.

## Puzzle 9: Deep Blue vs Kasparov

Rematch, Game 6, New York, May 11, 1997<br /> Caro-Kann Defence, Karpov Variation. Result: 1-0 (Kasparov resigned on move 19)

<Danger>
  **Played in the game: 7...h6?** Mistake
</Danger>

**Game line:** 7...h6 8.Nxe6! Qe7 9.O-O fxe6 10.Bg6+ Kd8

Pushing the h-pawn at once lets White play the knight sacrifice 8.Nxe6, which wrecks Black's king safety and cannot be undone. The theory move is 7...Bd6, and only after 8.O-O does ...h6 make sense. It is widely reported that Kasparov mixed up the move order, which is why this is remembered as an opening-preparation disaster rather than a deep strategic error.

## Puzzle 10: Kasparov vs Topalov

Hoogovens (round 4), Wijk aan Zee, January 20, 1999<br /> Pirc Defence, 150 Attack. Result: 1-0 (44 moves)

<Check>
  **Played in the game: 5...c6** Good move (flexible)
</Check>

**Game line:** 5...c6 6.f3 b5

A flexible reply to White's setup with Be3 and Qd2. It gives the queen the b6 square, prepares ...b5 and keeps Black's structure healthy, while Black waits to see how White will continue. It is a mainstream choice, so it is sound rather than risky. Kasparov's attacking plan came later.

## Puzzle 11: Kasparov vs Kramnik

Classical World Championship, Game 1, London, October 2000<br /> Ruy Lopez, Berlin Defence. Result: 1/2-1/2 (25 moves)

<Check>
  **Played in the game: 3...Nf6** Good move (solid)
</Check>

**Game line:** 3...Nf6 4.O-O Nxe4 5.d4 Nd6 6.Bxc6 dxc6 7.dxe5 Nf5 8.Qxd8+ Kxd8

Black attacks e4 at once and heads for the Berlin endgame. Black loses castling rights but gets the bishop pair and a very solid position, so queen trades come early. Kramnik used the Berlin to neutralise Kasparov, who could not win a single game with White against it in this match. Kasparov (White) has the better pawn structure, but Black is very hard to break down.
