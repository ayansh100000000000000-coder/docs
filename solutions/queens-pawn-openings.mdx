For each puzzle you get the move played in the game, how good it was, the game line that followed and the positional reasoning behind it. The ratings are teaching judgments from opening theory and game commentary, not engine evaluations.

## Puzzle 1: Botvinnik vs Capablanca

AVRO tournament (round 11), Netherlands, November 22, 1938<br /> Nimzo-Indian Defence, Botvinnik System. Result: 1-0 (41 moves)

<Check>
  **Played in the game: 9.Ne2!** Good move (accurate)
</Check>

**Game line:** 9.Ne2 b6 10.O-O Ba6 11.Bxa6 Nxa6 12.Bb2 Qd7

The knight goes to e2 instead of f3, so White keeps ideas of c4 and e4 and can later bring the knight to g3. Commentators mark the move with an exclamation point, and this game made the whole line popular. White's doubled c-pawns are not a problem here, because the bishop pair and the central pawns give White the better structure.

## Puzzle 2: Byrne vs Fischer

Rosenwald Memorial, New York, October 17, 1956<br /> Grünfeld Defence. Result: 0-1 (41 moves)

<Check>
  **Played in the game: 5...d5** Good move (thematic)
</Check>

**Game line:** 5...d5 6.Qb3 dxc4 7.Qxc4 c6

This is the key idea of the Grünfeld: Black allows White a big pawn centre and then hits it immediately with ...d5. The move is thematic and sound. The famous brilliancy in this game comes much later, but the opening choice itself was already ambitious and correct.

## Puzzle 3: Spassky vs Fischer

World Championship, Game 5, Reykjavik, July 1972<br /> Nimzo-Indian Defence, Hübner Variation. Result: 0-1 (27 moves)

<Check>
  **Played in the game: 6...Bxc3+** Good move (positional plan)
</Check>

**Game line:** 6...Bxc3+ 7.bxc3 d6 8.e4 e5 9.d5 Ne7

Black trades the bishop for the knight at once. White gets the bishop pair, but also doubled c-pawns. After 7.bxc3 d6 Black closes the centre with ...e5, so the bishops have little scope and White's pawns become targets. It is a classic positional idea, and it worked well for Fischer in this game.

## Puzzle 4: Fischer vs Spassky

World Championship, Game 6, Reykjavik, July 23, 1972<br /> Queen's Gambit Declined. Result: 1-0 (41 moves)

<Check>
  **Played in the game: 8.cxd5** Good move (main line)
</Check>

**Game line:** 8.cxd5 Nxd5 9.Bxe7 Qxe7 10.Nxd5 exd5

White releases the tension in the centre and starts trading pieces. It is a standard, well-known idea in this structure, so it is not a mistake. The result is a calm position where White has a small, safe pull and can play for a long game. This game is famous because Fischer, who nearly always played 1.e4, surprised Spassky by opening with 1.c4.

## Puzzle 5: Carlsen vs Nepomniachtchi

World Championship, Game 6, Dubai, December 3, 2021<br /> Queen's Pawn Game, Pseudo-Catalan. Result: 1-0 (136 moves)

<Check>
  **Played in the game: 6.b3** Good move (a sound surprise)
</Check>

**Game line:** 6.b3 c5 7.dxc5 Bxc5 8.c4 dxc4 9.Qc2 Qe7

A quiet, flexible setup: the bishop will aim down the long diagonal, and White has not yet committed the c-pawn. Wesley So called the move venomous but said Black should be okay, so it is a sound surprise and not an attack on theory. Carlsen won in 136 moves, the longest game in World Championship history.
